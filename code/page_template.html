What Britain drives

The most popular car makes, models and colours in every county, postcode area and nation, from the MOT test record of {{facts.GB.cars|m}} cars. Which places stand out, where electric cars have taken hold, and what is the average car where you live?

Dr Tristan Hotham, Independent researcher · 2026 · Data: DVSA anonymised MOT tests; DfT vehicle licensing statistics; ONS Postcode Directory (all Open Government Licence v3)

Every car in Great Britain over three years old must pass an MOT test each year, and the Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency publishes every test, stripped of anything personal, as open data. Each record carries the car's make, model, colour, fuel, engine size, the date it was first used and the postcode area of the garage that tested it. I streamed the whole 2025 file, {{meta.rows|int}} test records, and kept each vehicle once, at its last test of the year. That leaves {{meta.vehicles|int}} vehicles. MOT class 4 also covers light vans and pick-ups, so I set aside {{meta.vans|int}} of those ({{meta.van_share|pct1}}) by model, which leaves {{meta.cars|int}} cars, {{facts.GB.cars|int}} of them tested in a known postcode area.

Put them together and the average British car is a {{facts.GB.top_model_colour}} {{facts.GB.top_model}}, {{facts.GB.age|f1}} years old, with a {{facts.GB.cc|int}} cc engine: Ford is the most common make ({{facts.GB.top_make_share|pct1}} of cars), black the most common colour ({{facts.GB.top_colour_share|pct1}}) and the Fiesta, which Ford stopped making in 2023, still the most common model ({{facts.GB.fiesta_n|int}} cars, {{facts.GB.top_model_share|pct1}}). Blue is the Fiesta's own most common colour. The map shows how that picture changes from place to place.

Ford leads in {{facts.areas_ford|int}} of the {{facts.areas_n|int}} postcode areas, and in {{facts.counties_ford|int}} of the {{facts.counties_n_gb|int}} counties and unitary authorities of Great Britain. The exceptions cluster. Volkswagen takes {{facts.areas_volkswagen|int}} areas, mostly in the prosperous south of England around London. Vauxhall takes {{facts.areas_vauxhall|int}}: Luton, where Vauxhall built vehicles for more than a century, has the strongest showing of any make anywhere ({{facts.a_LU.top_make_share|pct1}} of cars), and the others are around Glasgow, Dundee and Falkirk, Teesside, Sunderland and the Wirral and Chester. Toyota leads in {{facts.areas_toyota|int}} areas, three in and around London, where the hybrid Toyota Prius is the most common model of all in east London, and the fourth in Shetland.     03 · Four nationsEngland, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland

    England's average car is a {{facts.England.top_model_colour}} {{facts.England.top_model}}, {{facts.England.age|f1}} years old. Wales is similar but even more of a Ford country ({{facts.Wales.top_make_share|pct1}} of cars are Fords) and more diesel ({{facts.Wales.diesel|pct1}} of tested cars, against {{facts.England.diesel|pct1}} in England). Scotland is different on three counts: its most common colour is white ({{facts.Scotland.top_colour_share|pct1}}, just ahead of black at {{facts.Scotland.colour2_share|pct1}}), its most common model is the Vauxhall Corsa, and Vauxhall runs Ford close ({{facts.Scotland.make2_share|pct1}} against {{facts.Scotland.top_make_share|pct1}}). Scotland's tested cars are also younger: {{facts.Scotland.age|f1}} years on average, against {{facts.England.age|f1}} in England and {{facts.Wales.age|f1}} in Wales. Against Great Britain as a whole, the make most over-represented in both Scotland and Wales is the budget brand Dacia ({{facts.Scotland.sig_lq|x1}} and {{facts.Wales.sig_lq|x1}} times its British share).

    England, Scotland and Wales: cars tested in 2025 (DVSA). Northern Ireland: licensed cars at the end of 2025, from DfT table VEH0120 (United Kingdom minus Great Britain); no colours are published.

    Northern Ireland needs a different source, because its MOTs are run by the Driver and Vehicle Agency, which publishes test totals and pass rates but, as far as I could find, no open data on makes or colours. The Department for Transport's licensing tables do cover the whole United Kingdom, so subtracting Great Britain from the UK gives Northern Ireland's {{facts.NI.cars|int}} licensed cars by make and model. They tell a distinct story: Volkswagen, not Ford, is the most common make ({{facts.NI.m1s|pct1}}), Audi is second ({{facts.NI.m2s|pct1}}, against {{facts.NI.gb_audi|pct1}} in Great Britain) and Ford only third. The Volkswagen Golf is the most common model ({{facts.NI.mod1s|pct1}}). Diesel still powers {{facts.NI.diesel|pct1}} of Northern Ireland's licensed cars, against {{facts.GB.dft_diesel|pct1}} in Great Britain.

    Britain's cars are dressed almost entirely in six colours: black, grey, white, blue, silver and red cover more than nine cars in ten.     05 · Odd ones outThe most unusual places

    To find the places whose cars look least like their nation's, I compared each place's mix of makes with its nation's and measured how much of the mix would have to change to match (the dissimilarity index, from 0 for identical to 1 for nothing in common). The most unusual postcode area in Britain is Shetland (ZE): {{facts.a_ZE.top_make_share|pct1}} of its cars are Toyotas, {{facts.unusual_area.0.lq|x1}} times Scotland's share, and the Yaris is its most common model. Next come London's postcode areas, where Toyota, Lexus and Mercedes-Benz stand out, perhaps reflecting the capital's large private hire trade, in which hybrid Toyotas are a familiar sight. In east London the Prius is the most common car of all ({{facts.a_E.top_model_share|pct1}} of cars).

    Counties with at least 15,000 tested cars. "Stands out" is the make with the highest ratio of local share to national share, among makes with at least 1% of the local cars. County mixes are blended from postcode areas, so these are differences of whole postcode areas.

    06 · ElectricWhere the electric cars are

    The MOT record is a poor guide to electric cars, because most are less than three years old and so have not yet had a test. For them I used the Department for Transport's licensing statistics, which count every licensed car by the keeper's neighbourhood across the whole United Kingdom. I counted privately kept cars only, because company and lease cars are registered at head-office addresses and pile up in a few places. At the end of 2025 there were {{facts.uk_bev|f1}} battery electric cars for every 100 privately kept cars in the UK. The highest rates are in central London: the City of London ({{facts.bev_top.0.bev|f1}} per 100), Camden ({{facts.bev_top.1.bev|f1}}) and Westminster ({{facts.bev_top.2.bev|f1}}). The lowest are in Northern Ireland, where Fermanagh and Omagh has {{facts.bev_low.0.bev|f1}} per 100, and the lowest in Great Britain is {{facts.bev_low_gb.0.name}}, the Western Isles ({{facts.bev_low_gb.0.bev|f1}}). Islands are not all alike: Orkney ({{facts.bev_scot.1.bev|f1}}) is second only to {{facts.bev_scot.0.name}} ({{facts.bev_scot.0.bev|f1}}) in Scotland, while Shetland has {{facts.bev_scot_shet|f1}}.

    How I did this

    MOT data. The DVSA publishes every MOT test as anonymised open data. I used the 2025 results file (twelve monthly files, {{meta.rows|int}} records), read straight from the publisher's server in the browser in pieces, so the 4.8 GB file was never stored. I kept MOT class 4 (cars, taxis, light vans and similar), and counted each vehicle once, at its latest test of 2025 ({{meta.retests|int}} earlier tests and retests of the same vehicles were dropped). Model names in the data mix short families ("FIESTA") with long trim names for newer cars, so I grouped them into families by rule. I set aside vans and pick-ups by family (Transit, Transporter, Berlingo, Ranger, Hilux and others) and by van-only makes; some car-derived vans will remain.

    Places. Each test carries the postcode area (the letters at the start of a postcode) of the garage, not the owner's address, so people who drive to a garage over a boundary are counted where the garage is. To get counties and unitary authorities, I shared each postcode area's cars out by the share of its live residential postcodes in each county (ONS Postcode Directory, August 2026, {{meta.postcodes|int}} postcodes). Nations are the sums of their counties. Postcode area outlines on the map are approximate: I drew them around the centres of postcodes grouped by neighbourhood (Voronoi cells, clipped to the coast).

    Electric cars and Northern Ireland. DfT vehicle licensing statistics count licensed cars (VEH0125) and plug-in vehicles (VEH0145) by neighbourhood for the whole UK; I used the end of 2025 and private keepers. Northern Ireland's makes and models are the UK total minus Great Britain in DfT table VEH0120.

    Limits. Cars under three years old have not had an MOT, so the MOT figures describe cars aged three and over: that is why the MOT count ({{facts.GB.cars|m}}) is below the {{meta.gb_licensed_cars|int}} cars licensed in Great Britain, and why the newest makes and electric cars are under-counted. Colour is the colour recorded when the car was registered. County figures are blends of postcode areas and should be read as broad patterns. "Average car" means the most common make, model and colour together with the mean age and engine size; few cars match all of these at once.
